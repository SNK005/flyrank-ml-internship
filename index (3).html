00ABSTRACT

Abstract

Large content libraries make manual page-by-page review impractical, so this study asks whether historical search-performance data can rank pages for review more effectively than a transparent, hand-built rule.

Using 176,738 pages from the FlyRank internship warehouse, March 2026 signals were used as features and April 2026's measured outcome as a forward-looking label, with a page counted as "declining" if its search impressions dropped more than 20% month over month. A Random Forest model was evaluated against a frozen rule-based baseline using a client-held-out split — 36 clients for training, 10 held out for testing, with zero client overlap. On the held-out clients, the model achieved a Precision@50 of 0.80 compared with 0.38 for the baseline — a 42-point improvement in top-of-queue ranking quality, though a ROC-AUC of 0.63 indicates only modest discrimination across the full population. The result is delivered as an auditable, ranked review queue intended to support editorial prioritization, not to automatically refresh, rewrite, or otherwise change any page.

KEY TERMS

Precision@50
Of the top 50 pages a method ranks as highest-risk, what fraction actually declined? A reviewer only has time to check so many pages — this measures whether the top of that list is worth their time.
ROC-AUC
A single score (0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect) for how well a model separates declining pages from stable ones across the entire population, not just at the top of the ranking.
Client-held-out split
The model was tested only on clients it never saw during training — the fairest way to check it isn't memorizing client-specific quirks instead of general page-level signal.

01INTRODUCTION / PROBLEM STATEMENT

Which pages deserve attention first?

A site with thousands of pages cannot be reviewed page-by-page with equal attention. The practical problem is one of prioritization: given what's already known about a page's recent performance, which pages should a reviewer look at first?

This capstone treats that prioritization problem as the operational case study for Lane 2 of the FlyRank ML Internship — using March 2026 search-performance signals to help decide which pages deserve investigation before April closes out.

The goal is deliberately narrow. This is not an attempt to reverse-engineer Google's ranking algorithm, nor a claim that any signal here causes a page to decline — it is a test of whether historical signals can improve a review queue over a transparent rule.

Why ranking, not automatic classification?

The workflow doesn't need the model to declare that a page must be refreshed — it's most useful if it places pages worth investigating near the top of a queue. This is why Precision@K is the primary metric: it directly answers "of the top K pages a reviewer would check, how many were worth checking?" — which mirrors the real constraint that a reviewer only has time for a short list.

A false positive here costs wasted review time. A false negative means a genuinely at-risk page goes unreviewed. The model is decision support; the final call stays human.

Exclusions and public-safety boundaries

Rows where gsc_data_available is not TRUE were excluded before aggregation (this flag can be NULL, not only true/false — filtering with IS TRUE avoids silently treating missing tracking as a real zero). Pages with no April record at all were excluded from the evaluated population, since they likely left tracking and cannot be assigned an honest outcome.

Client and content identifiers are pseudonymous and are never used as predictive features — only for joins and the grouped train/test split. This public paper contains no client names, domains, URLs, private queries, credentials, or raw warehouse exports.

03METHODOLOGY

Model, baseline, and validation

The workflow follows four principles: features drawn only from before the outcome period, an explicit and documented label definition, a transparent baseline that is frozen before any model is trained, and validation that keeps client identities fully separated between training and testing.

Label definition

Observed decline. A page is assigned is_declining_next_month = 1 when its April GSC impressions fall more than 20% below its March level, else 0. This mirrors the −20% threshold the underlying warehouse's own trend field uses, applied here as a genuine forward-looking outcome.

Predictive features (all from March, pre-outcome)

Transparent rule baseline (frozen — ML-07)

Before any model was trained, a rule-based ranking score was locked and never adjusted afterward: flag a page if March impressions ≥ 500, average position ≤ 10, and CTR < 1%, then rank qualifying pages by impressions. Freezing this rule before modeling is what keeps the later comparison fair — the baseline was never re-tuned to make the model look better.

Model selection

Three models were trained and compared on identical features and splits: Logistic Regression, a Decision Tree, and a Random Forest. Random Forest was selected as the final model on the strength of its ROC-AUC (0.63) — the best overall separation among the three, and it also achieved the strongest top-of-queue precision (Precision@50 of 0.80).

A row-level random split was deliberately avoided: pages from the same client tend to move together, so allowing a client's pages into both train and test could make performance look stronger than it would be on a genuinely unseen client. Because clients vary in size, the test set works out to 13% of rows even though it targets 20% of clients — an expected side effect of grouping, not an error.

Leakage audit

  • trend_direction / trend_pct are never used as features — they don't exist in the daily warehouse table used here, and are the same fields the old starter-dataset label was built from.
  • April's impressions are used only to construct the label, never as a model input.
  • content_hash_id was verified to map to exactly one client each (0 collisions found across 176,738 pages) — ruling out a join error that could have mixed one client's April outcome into another client's March row.

Error analysis

3,360 false positives and 5,500 false negatives were reviewed individually rather than judged by aggregate score alone.

FALSE POSITIVE — flagged as declining, wasn't

3,900 March impressions, 0 clicks → predicted decline probability 0.712, actual outcome: stable. Visible traffic with no clicks looks risky, but wasn't in this case — a reasonable miss rather than an obviously bad one.

FALSE NEGATIVE — missed a real decline

732 March impressions, position 12.2 → predicted decline probability 0.4999, actual outcome: declined. This sat right on the 0.50 decision line — evidence that March signals alone can't cleanly separate every borderline case.

05LIMITATIONS & HONEST FRAMING

What the evidence does — and does not — support

The result is correlational

The model identifies statistical association with the observed decline label. It does not demonstrate that any feature causes a page to decline.

One month-pair, one split

Validation uses a single forward window (March → April) and a single client-grouped split. This is not evidence the same performance holds across other months or future data releases.

Engagement signals are structurally weak in this slice

63.9% of pages had no GA4 tracking in March, so engagement-derived features could contribute little regardless of how predictive they might be in a fully-tracked population.

Modest overall discrimination

A ROC-AUC of 0.63 means the model is a prioritization aid, not a reliable predictor of any single page's outcome.

The baseline is intentionally simple

Beating this specific rule demonstrates improvement over this baseline — it does not establish superiority over a production-grade SEO prioritization system.

A refresh is not guaranteed to help

This analysis identifies pages that may deserve investigation. It does not test whether editing, rewriting, or otherwise refreshing a flagged page improves its search performance.

Claim ladder

OBSERVEDThe Random Forest achieved higher Precision@50 than the frozen baseline on the evaluated client-held-out split.
DIRECTIONALMarch search-performance signals may provide a useful ranking signal for content-review prioritization.
DECISION-SUPPORTThe score can help a reviewer decide which pages to check first.
NOT SUPPORTEDThis study does not prove Google's ranking algorithm, establish causality, or show that reviewing a ranked page will restore its traffic.

06RANKED RECOMMENDATIONS

The action playbook

The model score becomes useful only once it's translated into a manageable queue. Scored pages are grouped into three reviewer-facing bands by predicted decline probability — these bands are simple post-scoring rules, not additional model predictions.

BAND-01 · PROBABILITY ≥ 0.70

High-Priority Review

Pages the model considers most likely to decline. These should be the first pages a reviewer opens — inspect content, search intent, and recent performance before any action.

BAND-02 · PROBABILITY 0.50–0.70

Review

Meaningful but less certain risk. Worth a review pass once the high-priority band is cleared, weighing the page's actual content against the score.

BAND-03 · PROBABILITY < 0.50

Monitor

Lower predicted risk. Hold for a later review cycle rather than spending immediate editorial capacity here.

The model does not automatically rewrite, publish, delete, redirect, or modify pages. The human remains responsible for the final decision.

Human-review safeguards

  • Do not automatically rewrite, publish, delete, redirect, or otherwise modify a page solely because it receives a high score.
  • The score is one input into editorial prioritization, not a replacement for editorial judgment.
  • Sensitive-topic content (legal, medical, financial) warrants additional expert review beyond this scoring workflow.
  • Because validation covers one month-pair, scores should be treated as directional, and the model re-checked against new outcome windows as they become available before being trusted for ongoing use.
